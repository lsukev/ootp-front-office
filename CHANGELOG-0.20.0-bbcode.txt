[b][size=4]OOTP Front Office 0.20.0 — everything since 0.19.0[/size][/b]
[list]
[*][b]Fixed[/b] — Contract advice no longer recommends extending a player on the strength of his Value percentile alone. A user asked how those percentiles are worked out, having been told to open extension talks immediately with a long reliever carrying a 6.51 earned run average — "performing at a 93rd-percentile MLB value" — when nothing in the man's ratings or scouting reports suggested anything of the sort. He was right, and the word "performing" was the giveaway. OOTP's Value is worth to the club and playing time counts towards it: across the 280 major-league relievers checked, it tracked innings pitched at +0.37 and earned run average at +0.19 — the wrong way round, a worse ERA reading as slightly more valuable — while barely tracking the stuff rating at all. A converted starter soaking up long relief therefore floats to the top of the reliever pool whatever he does with the ball. Every recommendation to commit to a player now has to answer to the season as well. Where the results are clearly below the league it says hold off, and names the two facts that disagree; where the man has not played enough to judge it says that instead, because twenty innings is not evidence against anybody.
[*][b]New[/b] — The season line travels with the percentile everywhere it goes. Each player carries one league-relative number — wRC+ for a bat, ERA+ for an arm, with the innings or plate appearances behind it — on the Contracts page and in what the assistants read. The briefing described a value percentile as performance because it had been handed the percentile and no statistics at all; it now has the line, and every prompt that carries these figures says plainly what they are and what they are not.
[*][b]Fixed[/b] — Percentiles are taken against major leaguers only. The pool was every player carrying a major-league club's id, and OOTP parks a signing nobody has assigned yet on the parent club with no roster spot at all — the same thing that once put teenagers from the international complex in the major-league column of the depth chart. Of the 309 arms being called major-league relievers in one save, 29 averaged twenty years old, a stuff rating of 26 and a value of 254 against the real men's 841. A yardstick partly made of children reads long, and every percentile in the app was high because of it — worst at the bottom of the league, where the weakest arms on real rosters were reading 7 and 8 when they are ahead of none of it.
[/list]
